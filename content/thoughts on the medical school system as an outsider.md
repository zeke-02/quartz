# The rant
There is no repeatable formula for innovation. However, one prerequisite that I'm certain of is the existence of inventors. Now, this is a pretty ill-defined group of individuals, but my intuition tells me that for all their wonderful variety, they hold two traits in common; curiosity and obsession. From this, one can subsequently produce a litmus-test for different fields and measure the extent to which they promote those two traits within the people working in them.

Applying our methods to the domain of medicine, it appears to me that the current institutions do not cultivate the qualities necessary for innovation within individuals, and as a result the innovation that we will see won't come from clinicians working in the system, but from unconventional outsiders.

This is the confluence of a few decade-old trends.

# Trends
First, let's consider the selection of the individuals who are put through 10+ years of schooling to attain the highest expertise in their discipline. Ahem, I don't think the medical school system actually selects for the raw obsession and curiosity needed to drive actual progress in the medical field. [Look](https://hms.harvard.edu/education-admissions/graduation) at our top institutions and the individuals produced from them. Most of them are working on more equitable social health outcomes and marginal improvements to patient care. Maybe there are young, crazy innovators in the medical field, I just can't seem to find *any*.

What about the current state of working doctors?

New doctors simply have not put in enough time to develop a competency and intuition that rivals the previous generation. This is partly due to the fact that excessive resident hours have been curtailed, and most new entrants now [prioritize](https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/careers/young-doctors-want-work-life-balance-older-doctors-say-thats-not-the-job-6cb37d48) a balanced lifestyle. In addition, the drive from insurance companies to squeeze the numerous afflictions of the human body and their respective treatments into a sort of "checkbox medicine" impedes critical thinking and a deep engagement with the very gaps in our knowledge of medicine. Clinical experience is vital for medical breakthroughs and is part of the reason why many radical medical breakthroughs in the past were physician-led.

# Ok so what?
So what if doctors are becoming more like unthinking cogs?
My guess is that we will still see improvements in fields where clinical experience isn't as necessary, but progress will plateau when it comes to actually treating physical, time-sensitive injuries through surgical intervention. While I hope I’m wrong, the current trends don’t inspire much optimism. If we want our children to benefit from life-saving procedures that don’t exist today, we may need to look **outside the conventional system**—to biohackers, rogue scientists, and bored engineers.

# Other thoughts that didn't fit in
- The primacy of insurance companies, has largely been the consequences of government mangling the natural market equilibrium between original providers of care, doctors and nurses, and patients. 
- I don't see how improvements to surgical robots will actually generate novel treatments because platforms like the Da Vinci mostly assist surgeons rather than serving as general platforms for new operations.
