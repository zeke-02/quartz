Now barring the creation of an AI scientist, I predict a either a slowdown in medical innovation over the next coming decade or that the innovation that we do see in medicine will come from outside the polished, implausibly quirky products of the medical school system, possibly from people like Josie Zayner and the biohacker movement.

Why? It's a confluence of a few trends.

First, there are simply fewer doctors coming out of medical school because of the economic cost and time [commitment](https://jakeseliger.com/2012/10/20/why-you-should-become-a-nurse-or-physicians-assistant-instead-of-a-doctor-the-underrated-perils-of-medical-school/) of the [medical school process](https://jakeseliger.com/2012/10/20/why-you-should-become-a-nurse-or-physicians-assistant-instead-of-a-doctor-the-underrated-perils-of-medical-school/). 

Second, the quality of doctors is decreasing. Most of the new batch of doctors coming out of the pipeline have simply not put in enough time to develop a competency that rivals the previous generation, and are eager to work as employees with set hours and pay. More striking to me, I don't think that the archetypal mad scientist/doctor would not be applying to medical school today. Most young individuals who are applying to medical school are drawn to it either out of prestige, money, and fear of the unknown because it's a "clearly defined, well-lit path". Do those filters select for people obsessively trying to creatively solve real problems? I don't think so. Older doctors just seem incredibly obsessed with medicine, new ones seem obsessed with the idea of being a doctor.

Third, working doctors operating in healthcare networks are handed a predetermined checklist of operations that are covered by insurance that they are allowed to perform, which does not encourage critical thinking or novel approaches to treatment. By forcibly inserting insurance companies as the suppliers of medicine instead of the actual providers of care (doctors, nurses, etc.), we have disrupted natural incentives to innovate.

Finally, the system is hostile to real risk and since innovation involves risk, it is hostile to innovation. This has been covered by Balaji and most libertarians.

A short anecdote to illustrate the last point.
I know an orthopedic surgeon who has spent over two decades of his life building a small medical device startup from scratch that has yet to find traction. The device? A rudimentary stem cell implant device that  amounts to a metallic screw the diameter of a few centimeters. He has poured countless late nights working with cadavers at a local hospital, photocopying technical papers, and dreaming of new applications. He has a drawer of hundreds of iterations of this screw; some prototypes were barely 10mm and made of a cheap, plastic material, while others were several centimeters and made of smooth metal. He also came up with a compendium of new, simple surgical techniques to use for this implant.

Now, one would imagine that 21st inventors, spend most of their time honing their designs, experimenting new ideas, and relentlessly chasing incremental improvements. This is not the case. After speaking with his medical device engineer, I realized that they spend a majority of their time navigating the byzantine world of FDA approval. The fate of his life's work comes down to convincing some PhD who's never been inside an OR that his device is safe, tough luck.

Anyways, hopefully I'm wrong and my kids will have access to life-saving procedures that didn't exist before.
