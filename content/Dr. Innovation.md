How does progress in medicine occur in 2024? Who specifically is testing out new ideas? Bringing those ideas to the hospital? Directly improve treatment?

All things being equal, the individuals that are closest to the ground-truths of a given problem are the most-likely to improve it. In this case, one would think that those who are directly confronted with the shortcomings and miracles of both the human body and modern medicine in their daily life, doctors and nurses, are poised to ideate upon received wisdom.
 
Now barring the creation of an AI scientist, I predict a either a slowdown in medical innovation over the next coming decade or that the innovation that we do see in medicine will come from outside the polished, implausibly quirky products of the medical school system, possibly from people like Josie Zayner and the biohacker movement.

Hot takes indeed from the 20 something; I have spoken to quite a few older doctors that are nearing their retirement, current residents, and college premeds, but you should think for yourself.

Why the slowdown? It is a combination of a few trends.

First, there are simply less doctors coming out of medical school because of the economic cost and time [commitment](https://jakeseliger.com/2012/10/20/why-you-should-become-a-nurse-or-physicians-assistant-instead-of-a-doctor-the-underrated-perils-of-medical-school/) of the medical school process. Second, the quality of doctors is decreasing and they aren't incentivized to innovate when they are essentially cogs to some giant insurance corporation. The natural market, patients paying for better care from doctors, has been disrupted by policies like Medicare and the push for healthcare as a human right, which ironically decreases the quality and cost of care. This means that insurance companies, middle men, have become the suppliers of medicine, and their incentives aren't aligned with the actual providers and consumers of care, doctors and their patients. For each patient, doctors in a given healthcare network are handed a predetermined checklist of operations that are covered by insurance that they are allowed to perform, which does not encourage critical thinking or novel approaches to treatment. In addition, more new doctors are eager to work as employees with a "well-balanced" life and since they put work-hour restrictions on medical students the amount of time that prospective doctors can actually dedicate to their craft is capped. Most of the new batch of doctors coming out of the pipeline have simply not put in enough time to develop a competency that rivals the previous generation.

Another factor that I weight quite heavily is that the archetypal mad scientist would not be applying to medical school today. Most young individuals who are applying to medical school are drawn to it either out of prestige, money, and fear of the unknown because it's a "clearly defined, well-lit path". Do those filters select for people trying to creatively solve real problems? I don't think so. Older doctors just seem incredibly obsessed with medicine, new ones seem obsessed with the idea of being a doctor.

Third, the system is hostile to real risk and since innovation involves risk, it is hostile to innovation. This has been covered by Balaji and most libertarians.

I know an orthopedic surgeon who has spent over two decades of his life building a small medical device startup from scratch that has yet to find traction. The device? A rudimentary stem cell implant device that essentially amounts to a metallic screw the diameter of a few centimeters. He has poured countless late nights working with cadavers at a local hospital, photocopying technical papers, and dreaming of new applications. I distinctly remember finding in our kitchen a drawer of hundreds of iterations of this screw; some prototypes were barely 10mm and made of a cheap, plastic material, while others were several centimeters and made of smooth metal. He also came up with a compendium of new, simple surgical techniques to use for this implant. 
Now, one would imagine that 21st inventors, like my father, spend most of their time honing their designs, experimenting new ideas, and relentlessly chasing incremental improvements. This is not the case. After speaking with his medical device engineer, I realized that they spend a majority of their time navigating the byzantine world of FDA approval. The fate of his life's work comes down to convincing some PhD who's never been inside an OR that his device is safe, tough luck.
